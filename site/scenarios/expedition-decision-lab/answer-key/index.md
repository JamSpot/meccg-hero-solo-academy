# Expedition Decision Lab — Model Answers

These are model reasoning patterns, not a single mandatory tactical answer. Your actual card hand, wounds, taps, printed text, and selected solo procedure can change the decision.

## Case 1 — The first expedition

- The route Rhudaur → Hollin → Redhorn Gate contains **three regions**, including the regions containing the origin and destination.
- Check every adjacent pair against the physical region cards before declaring it.
- In the teaching example, all three regions are Wilderness, so the route exposes the company to creatures whose printed keying can use the required Wilderness symbols. A particular creature is not automatically legal: check its full key and all conditions.
- The site path is used for Hazard keying; it does not itself determine movement. Region Movement legality comes from the actual region sequence and adjacency.
- Under the Academy's labelled solo procedure, Moria's site number of 3 produces a draw of **2 + (2 × 3) = 8**. The five-character company's base Hazard Limit is **5**. Keep these separate.
- Before attempting Book of Mazarbul, reassess the company's wounds/taps, the resources remaining, the site's automatic attack, and the value of the objective relative to the risk.

**Decision principle:** a legal route can still be strategically expensive. State the likely exposure before you move.

## Case 2 — The hand is not the plan

There is no universal discard answer without the exact hand and printed card text. A sound answer explains the next objective and what each retained card contributes.

- **Needed now:** cards that support the immediate movement, defence, or site attempt and are legal in the current situation.
- **Needed later:** cards whose conditions depend on a future site, character, objective, or game state.
- **Low value for this plan:** cards with no plausible role in the current campaign line, after checking their text.

A card can be temporarily unplayable but valuable later. Conversely, holding every card can prevent useful hand turnover and obscure the plan. **Thror's Map**, for example, should be judged by its actual printed effect and the future expedition you are planning—not kept automatically because it sounds useful.

**Decision principle:** keep a card for a named future use, not for vague possibility.

## Case 3 — Eight drawn, five is the limit

Eight is the number of Hazard cards available to consider under the Academy's chosen solo draw procedure. Five is the base number of Hazard cards that may be played against this five-character company in its Movement/Hazard Phase, subject to the actual rules and card effects.

Check each card for keying, target, timing, conditions, and whether it counts against the Hazard Limit. The solo procedure asks the Hazard player to choose the strongest disadvantageous **legal** combination within the limit; it does not make illegal cards playable. If fewer than five candidates are legal, play only the legal ones. Do not assume two support cards stack until the exact wording and relevant rulings confirm it.

**Decision principle:** legality first, then sequence and impact, then count the limit.

## Case 4 — Arrive, then decide

The company is in a weakened state, but that alone does not prove the site attempt is wrong. Check the printed site card, automatic attack, remaining resources, and actual combat capability.

A strong answer compares:
- the value and urgency of attempting the objective now;
- the likely cost to the company;
- the remaining legal defensive or avoidance options;
- the cost of delaying or recovering;
- and the future position created by each choice.

**Decision principle:** explain the trade-off using the current state. There is no automatic “enter” or “retreat” rule.

## Case 5 — The next expedition is not automatic

The proposed direct route has **five regions**, including origin and destination, so it exceeds the normal four-region limit for one Region Movement.

The verified teaching example divides it into two legs:
1. Moria → Rivendell: Redhorn Gate → Hollin → Rhudaur.
2. Rivendell → Blue Mountain Dwarf-hold: Rhudaur → Arthedain → Númeriador.

Check adjacency against the physical region cards before moving. Rivendell is a useful intermediate Haven in this example, but it is not mandatory for every campaign. An alternative stop is acceptable only after its site availability and route legality are checked.

**Decision principle:** an intermediate stop can make the route legal and create time to recover, manage the hand, and reconsider the objective. It is a strategic choice, not simply wasted movement.

## Case 6 — Same destination, different exposure

Both routes must first pass the legality test. Then read the actual creature card: the presence of a matching region type is only one part of creature keying, and named-region/site requirements or other conditions may also matter.

Compare the routes from both sides:
- **Hero:** objective value, survival, resources spent, arrival condition, and next position.
- **Hazard:** legal threats available, likely impact, and the company's vulnerable characters or objective.

A route that reduces immediate exposure may leave the company poorly positioned; a route that improves the next leg may invite a legal threat now. Neither route is universally best.

**Decision principle:** choose based on the whole expedition, not only the next Hazard Phase.

## Case 7 — Three-game evidence

Do not conclude that a card is weak because it did not appear in one game. Separate the result into “not seen,” “seen but illegal,” “legal but low impact,” and “useful in a confirmed situation.” Keep the baseline list unchanged for all three tests.

If you want to alter a card quantity later, record:
1. the repeated situation where it helped or failed;
2. the relevant card text and ruling;
3. the alternative card you would use;
4. what you expect the change to improve;
5. and the next test that could disprove your theory.

**Decision principle:** test one meaningful change at a time. A repeatable baseline makes the result interpretable.
