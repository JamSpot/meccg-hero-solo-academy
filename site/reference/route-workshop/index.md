# Route Workshop — From Map to Decision

This is the practical follow-on to [Free / Player-Directed Movement]({{ '/reference/free-movement/' | relative_url }}). Keep the Region cards or a legal region map beside you. Do not rely on memory when checking adjacency.

The objective is not to memorise one route. It is to learn a repeatable decision process.

## The six checks

Use these in order whenever a company plans to move.

1. **Mission:** What am I trying to achieve at the destination?
2. **Movement type:** Is Starter Movement, Region Movement, or another movement type legal here?
3. **Route legality:** Do the endpoints, adjacency, region count and no-repeat rule all work?
4. **Site path:** What regions and region types will be declared for Hazard keying?
5. **Expedition risk:** What legal Hazards become more attractive because of this path?
6. **Next position:** What can the company do after arriving, and what condition might it be in?

A route that fails check 3 is not an option. A route that passes check 3 is merely legal; it is not automatically good.

## Worked example 1 — Rivendell to Moria

**Mission:** Acquire Book of Mazarbul.  
**Origin:** Rivendell.  
**Destination:** Moria.  
**Movement type:** Region Movement.

A legal teaching route is:

**Rhudaur → Hollin → Redhorn Gate**

Check it against the map:

- Rhudaur contains Rivendell, so it is the origin region.
- Redhorn Gate contains Moria, so it is the destination region.
- The regions connect in the declared sequence.
- There are three regions, counting both endpoints.
- No region is repeated.

The declared site path includes the region names and their types. The names matter because some creatures can be keyed to named regions; the types matter because other creatures are keyed to region types.

**Captain's decision:** This route reaches the objective in one Region Movement, but it also tells the Hazard player more named-region information than a legal Starter Movement route would. Choose it because the expedition benefit justifies that exposure—not simply because it is possible.

## Worked example 2 — The tempting route that is too long

Consider the alternative:

**Rhudaur → High Pass → Anduin Vales → Wold & Foothills → Redhorn Gate**

The route follows connected regions, but it contains five regions.

Normal Region Movement permits no more than four consecutive regions, counting the origin and destination. Therefore this route is **illegal for one normal Region Movement**.

Do not try to repair an illegal route by arguing that the company is only crossing three intermediate regions. The origin and destination count too.

**Lesson:** Check the count before you assess the route's strategic value.

## Worked example 3 — A route that repeats a region

Suppose a proposed path reads:

**A → B → C → B → D**

Even if every individual connection appears on the map, the route repeats B. Normal Region Movement does not permit a repeated region in the sequence.

**Lesson:** Adjacency alone does not prove legality. Check the entire sequence.

## Starter Movement versus Region Movement

Starter Movement is not a universal shortcut to any destination. It is available only when the relevant site cards and nearest-Haven conditions allow it. Its site path is determined by the printed site-path information and the origin/destination regions, rather than by naming every intervening region.

Region Movement gives the player more control over the route, but all regions in the route are named for the site path. That extra control can also create more named-region Hazard opportunities.

Before choosing, ask:

- Is Starter Movement actually legal between these sites?
- If not, is Region Movement legal on the map?
- What site path will each legal choice create?
- Which route better supports the objective and the company's next position?

Do not assume that Starter Movement is always safer or that Region Movement is always better. The map, the site cards, the company's mission and the available Hazard cards all matter.

## The route scorecard

Use this table for any proposed route. Fill it in before revealing the new site.

| Check | Your answer | Pass condition |
|---|---|---|
| Mission | What do we want at the destination? | A specific purpose |
| Movement type | What movement are we using? | The rules permit it |
| Origin and destination | Which regions contain the two sites? | They are the correct endpoints |
| Adjacency | Is each consecutive pair connected? | Every connection is legal |
| Region count | How many regions, including endpoints? | Four or fewer for normal Region Movement |
| Repeats | Does any region appear twice? | No repeated region |
| Site path | What names and types are exposed? | Declared correctly |
| Hazard plan | What could punish this path? | At least one likely risk identified |
| Arrival | What must be true to attempt the objective? | Company can make a deliberate entry decision |
| Next position | What useful action follows? | A plausible follow-up or recovery plan |

If a legality check fails, stop and redesign the route. Do not continue to risk assessment as though the movement were valid.

## Compare routes by the whole expedition

When two legal routes reach the same destination, do not choose by distance alone. Compare:

- **Exposure:** which named regions and region types are available for creature keying?
- **Company condition:** can the company tolerate the likely pressure?
- **Objective:** how much value is at the destination?
- **Entry and attempt:** is the company prepared to face the site's challenges?
- **Future position:** where can it go next?
- **Recovery:** if the expedition goes badly, what is the sensible fallback?

There may be no universally correct route. The goal is to be able to explain why you chose one.

## Practice drill — do it with the physical map

For each proposed route, write down the movement type, endpoint regions, region count, repeat check and intended site path.

### Drill A — Four regions

A → B → C → D

**Question:** Is the count within the normal limit?

**Answer:** Yes, if the endpoints, adjacency and no-repeat checks are also valid.

### Drill B — Five regions

A → B → C → D → E

**Question:** Is it legal for normal Region Movement?

**Answer:** No. Five regions exceeds the four-region maximum.

### Drill C — Repeated region

A → B → C → B → D

**Question:** Is it legal?

**Answer:** No. B is repeated.

### Drill D — Legal but risky

Two routes are both legal. Route One exposes fewer useful creature-keying opportunities. Route Two exposes more, but puts the company in a better position for its next objective.

**Question:** Which route is better?

**Answer:** Compare the likely disruption with the value of the follow-up position and the company's ability to withstand the extra pressure. There is no automatic winner.

### Drill E — Destination without a mission

You can reach a site, but cannot explain what the company will gain by going there.

**Question:** Should you move just because it is reachable?

**Answer:** Usually not. Identify the reward, campaign purpose or positional benefit first. If none exists, reconsider the destination.

## The Captain's spoken route declaration

Before moving, say:

> My destination is ______ because ______.

> I am using ______ movement.

> My route is ______.

> The route is legal because ______.

> The site path exposes ______.

> The main risk is ______.

> After arrival, I want to ______.

> If the company is too damaged, I will ______.

This short script makes route planning visible. It also gives you a consistent way to review a decision after the game.
